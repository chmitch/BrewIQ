# Chemical composition, aroma profile, antioxidant activity, and sensory attributes of beer according to roasting degree and malt type



- Source: PubMed Central PMC12552634 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12552634/)

- Authors: Seung Min Yu, Da Hye Song, Yu-Rim Lee, Ki Nam Kim, Sae-Hun Kim, Jeung Hee An et al.

- Journal: Food Chemistry: X (2025)

- DOI: 10.1016/j.fochx.2025.103147

- License: https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-nd/4.0/

- Relevance to BrewIQ: malt type and roast degree



## Abstract

This study examined the effects of malt roasting degree on the chemical composition, aroma profile, antioxidant activity, and sensory attributes of brewed beer. Roasting increased carbohydrate content, diastatic power, and Maillard products such as melanoidins and Amadori compounds, contributing to deeper color and enhanced antioxidant activity. Aroma profiling with an electronic nose revealed that roasting shifted key volatile classes, including esters, terpenes, pyrazines, and sulfur compounds. Light malt beer retained fruity esters (for example, ethyl hexanoate and cis-3-hexenyl acetate), whereas dark malt beer was enriched in Maillard-derived pyrazines and aldehydes (for example, 2,3-dimethylpyrazine, furfural, maltol) that impart roasted and caramelized notes. Liquid malt beer showed the greatest loss of monoterpenes (for example, γ-terpinene), indicating thermal degradation. These changes aligned with antioxidant capacity, as melanoidin- and pyrazine-rich profiles enhanced radical scavenging. Overall, roasting modulated sensory and antioxidant properties through Maillard-derived transformations, underscoring their impact on beer quality.




### Introduction

Beer is the most consumed alcoholic beverage in the world (Calvo-Porral, 2019). Unlike traditional fermented beverages, such as sake, which undergo parallel fermentation, beer production follows a distinct process in which saccharification, and fermentation occur sequentially. During beer brewing, enzymes in the malt first break down starch into reducing sugars, followed by yeast fermentation, which converts maltose and glucose into alcohol and carbon dioxide (Kim, 2020; Park & Kang, 2020). During saccharification, alpha- and beta-amylases convert the barley starch into glucose. Alcohol is produced by yeast during fermentation, organic acids are produced by microorganisms, such as lactic acid bacteria, and flavor components impart taste and aroma. Beer quality depends on the wort, byproducts, yeast type, and fermentation conditions (Lee et al., 2019). Furthermore, various malts, including roasted malts, can be added to enhance beer flavor and color (Féchir et al., 2021). Castro et al. (2021) reported that the substitution of 20 % base barley malt with various specialty malts during wort production influenced the composition and properties of the wort and beer. Furthermore, the aroma of caramelized wheat malt beer is characterized by the presence of furanones, pyranone, maltol, and pyrazines. Roasted wheat malt beer is characterized by smoky and phenolic compounds, specifically 2-methoxyphenol and 4-methylphenol (Reglitz et al., 2022). Recent studies have also shown that thermally processed malts deliver distinct phenolic acids, flavonoids, and melanoidins that influence both antioxidant activity and sensory attributes of beer (Nardini & Garaguso, 2020; Shopska et al., 2021). Comparative analyses further suggest that higher roasting intensity promotes the formation of Maillard reaction products and relates with greater antioxidant potential, linking malt color and phenolic composition (Carvalho et al., 2016).

Especially, the functional ingredients of beer include B vitamins, minerals, antioxidant polyphenols, ethanol, dietary fiber, and prebiotic components (Bamforth, 2009; Sohrabvandi et al., 2010), which have been shown to benefit human health by providing nutritional, antimutagenic, and anticancer effects; reducing cardiovascular disease; antihyperlipidemic effects; stimulating the immune system; anti-osteoporosis effects; and reducing the risk of dementia (Gémes et al., 2016; Sohrabvandi et al., 2012). Functional interest in beer is growing due to its low ethanol content and richness in antioxidant compounds. Recently, various beer components also have been reported to exhibit antioxidant activity. Schwarz et al. (2012) studied the effect of mashing temperature on polyphenol release. The optimal temperature for the release of phenolic acids from malt was determined to be within the range of 40–45 °C. However, no enzymatic activity associated with phenolic acid release was found at temperatures above 65 °C. Fumi et al. (2011) examined the fate of polyphenols in pure wort and corn wort during key brewing steps. Total phenol content was lower in maize-adjunct, and a 50 % decrease in phenol content during the entire brewing process has been reported. Zhao (2015) investigated the impact of processing steps on the profile of phenolic compounds from malt to the final product and found that their amounts were significantly enhanced during malting and mashing but then reduced during fermentation and storage. Pascoe et al. (2003) reported that antioxidant activity increased throughout the brewing process, including mashing, boiling, fermentation, and cold storage. Beyond total phenolics, melanoidins generated during kilning and roasting act as potent antioxidants through radical scavenging, reducing, and metal-chelating mechanisms, explaining antioxidant gains at higher thermal loads (Martinez-Gomez et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2025). While the relationship between phenolic compounds and antioxidant activity in beer has been broadly documented (Osorio-Paz et al., 2020; Piazzon et al., 2010), most studies have addressed overall levels rather than specific transformations during malt processing. However, studies that mechanistically trace how phenolic subclasses originating from different malt types—especially thermally processed malts—are transformed during kilning/roasting, mashing, fermentation, and storage, and how these transformations map onto antioxidant capacity, are still limited (Koren et al., 2019; Nardini & Garaguso, 2020; Shopska et al., 2021).

In this study, we investigated the antioxidant activity during malt roasting and brewing through an increase in phenolic compounds and Maillard reaction products and attempted to determine the optimal processing factor for the quantity of radical scavenger compounds. We focused on the influence of the roasting steps on the content and antioxidant activity of phenolic compounds, flavonoids, terpenes, tannins, and other bioactive compounds. By combining functional analysis and sensory evaluation, this study provides a comprehensive understanding of how malt processing influences both the antioxidant properties and flavor profile of beer. Therefore, our study demonstrates that an increase in Maillard reaction products and aroma compounds is associated with enhanced antioxidant activity.


### Materials and methods


#### Production of roasting malts and liquid malt

Barley-based malts (Haenamgun, South Korea) were roasted to produce various types of malts. Steeping was performed by soaking the barley in water for 8 h to attain a moisture content of 45–48 %. Barley maintained a 94 % germination rate during the germination process and subsequently dried. This was based on the findings of a preliminary study (Kim et al., 2021). Samples were dried at 65 °C for 1 h, followed by 3 h at 70 °C until the sample reached the desired consistency. Light malt was cured at 93 °C for 3 h, dark malt at 107 °C for 3 h, and 121 °C for 1 h. To produce the liquid malt, base-malted barley was mixed with water to create a mash. The water-to-malt ratio was 3:1. After subsequent mashing at 63 °C for 90 min, the mixture was concentrated using a rotary evaporator under reduced pressure to reduce mass and increase concentration. The final liquid-malt content was 75°Brix.


#### Production of beer made with various malts

The base malt was ground as 100 % base malt, the light malt was ground in a ratio of 1.9:1 (base malt:light malt), and the dark malt was ground in a ratio of 1.9:1 (base malt:dark malt). The water/ground malt ratio was 3:1 (w/w). Mashing was carried out in a water bath at a constant temperature of 63 °C for 40 min. The mash was then boiled for 40 min. Twenty g/15 L of hops (Simcoe, Yakima Hop, Washington, USA) was added 10 min before boiling. The beer was then cooled with cold water in a 20 °C fermenter and fermented with 5 g/15 L of Saccharomyces cerevisiae (US-05; Fermentis, Belgium) at 20 °C for 5 d. The final parameters were 97 % for the base beer, 81 % for the light beer, and 80 % for the dark and liquid beer (Mosher & Trantham, 2017).


#### Proximate analysis of malts and beers

The proximate compositions of various malts were determined using the AOAC method (AOAC International, 2000) for moisture, crude/malt protein, crude lipids, crude fiber, and ash (AOAC: 925.10, 950.36, 963.15, 973.18, and 942.05, respectively). The carbohydrate content was calculated as follows: 100 – (moisture content + lipid content + protein content+ crude fiber content+ crude ash content).


#### Germination

Following the European Brewery Convention (EBC) method (European Brewing Convention, 2023) 3.6.2, 100 kernels were placed in 90-mm Petri dishes lined with two layers of Whatman filter paper moistened with 10 mL of distilled water. The samples were incubated in a germination chamber set at 20 °C with a relative humidity of 95 %. Germination was monitored from 24 h to 48 h, with a final count at 72 h.


#### Husk content

The husk content was determined following the procedure outlined by Whitmore (1960).


#### Kolbach index (ratio of soluble and total protein)

Various malt-soluble nitrogen was analyzed by measuring the soluble protein and total protein content in the malt, following EBC method 4.9. The Kolbach's index was calculated using the formula specified in the EBC method (2023):

Kolbach index = (% Soluble protein / % Total protein) × 100.


#### Spectrophotometric determination of diastatic power

Diastatic power was determined using the EBC 4.12 method with minor modifications. A starch solution (2 % w/v, 20 mL) was prepared in 20 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 4.6) and equilibrated at 30 °C for 10 min in Pyrex Corning tubes (25 × 150 mm). Filtered malt extract (0.2 mL) was added to a starch solution, and each mixture was vigorously stirred using a vortex mixer. Reactions were incubated at 40 °C for 60 min. N sodium hydroxide solution (2 mL) was added to terminate each reaction, and each mixture was vigorously stirred. The resulting solutions were stored at 4 °C for further analysis. Reaction blanks were prepared by adding 2 mL of 0.5 N sodium hydroxide solution to 20 mL of starch solution (2 % w/v) in 20 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 4.6), followed by addition of 0.2 mL of malt extract with thorough mixing. The absorbance of each mixture was measured at 460 nm and diastatic power was calculated using the following formula:

Diastatistic power (as-is), W-K = (569.6903775 × optical density at 460 nm) + 44.06273324.

Diastatistic power (d-b), W-K = as-is × 100/(100-moisture content).


#### pH

The pH of various malts was measured (HI 8424 pH meter; HANNA Instruments, Cluj-Napoca, Romania) after mixing 4 g of malt powder in 40 mL of distilled water under shaking for 5 min.


#### Total sugar content in malt

The total carbohydrate content of the samples was quantified using the phenol‑sulfuric acid method, a well-established colorimetric assay (DuBois et al., 1956). Each sample (1 g) was dispersed in 100 mL of distilled water by vortexing and subjected to shaking for 24 h. Subsequently, 200 μL of the sample solution was collected, to which 200 μL of 5 % phenol reagent and 1 mL of 95 % sulfuric acid were added. The mixture was incubated in the dark for 20 min, and absorbance was recorded at 490 nm. Glucose was used as the standard for calibration.


#### Amadori compounds and melanoidins analysis

To prepare the malt extract, 10 g of ground malt was mixed with 100 mL of distilled water and extracted at 100 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, the extract was filtered. The beer was measured directly without further processing. The Maillard reaction products in beer and malt were measured according to the methods that have been previously reported (Bravo et al., 2012). Briefly, 100 μL of beer and malt extracts were diluted with distilled water to a final volume of 4 mL. The UV spectrometer (INNO, LTEK, Republic of Korea) was then utilized to measure the optical density of Amadori compounds (at 304 nm) and melanoidins (at 420 nm) in all samples (beer and malt extracts).


#### Beer quality parameters


#### Color of malt and beer

The color of the beer was determined using the EBC 9.6 method.


#### Alcohol by volume (ABV) of beer

The alcohol content (% ABV) of beer was determined by distillate density using the EBC 9.2 method.


#### Yeast viability

Yeast viability (colony-forming units, CFU/mL) in beer was determined in triplicate on potato dextrose agar (Kisan Bio Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea) incubated aerobically at 25 °C.


#### Free amino acid analysis in beer

Free amino acids were analyzed using a Hitachi L-8900 high-speed amino acid analyzer (Hitachi High-Tech, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a cation-exchange column (#2622PF, 4.6 mm i.d. × 60 mm) maintained at 57 °C. Samples were deproteinized with 5 % trichloroacetic acid (TCA, 1:1, v/v) and centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 20 min); the supernatant was treated with n-hexane to remove lipids and pigments, passed through a 0.2 μm syringe filter, and diluted with 0.02 M HCl. For samples containing high glutamine levels, an additional 30-fold dilution was applied after preliminary ninhydrin-based screening to prevent column overloading. Separation was performed with a PF buffer gradient (Kanto Chemical, Tokyo, Japan), followed by post-column derivatization with ninhydrin reagent (Wako, Osaka, Japan) at 55 °C. Detection was carried out at 570 nm for primary amines and 440 nm for secondary amines, and chromatographic data were processed using EZChrom Elite software. In this study, the analysis targeted a panel of free amino acids, including threonine, serine, asparagine, glutamic acid, glycine, alanine, leucine, tyrosine, phenylalanine, histidine, lysine, arginine, and proline, as well as amino acid–related compounds such as cystathionine, ethanolamine, γ-aminobutyric acid, ornithine, and ammonia. Calibration curves were constructed using commercial standard mixtures (Type B and AN-II, Wako) with correlation coefficients (R2) ≥ 0.995, and concentrations were expressed as mg/L or mg/100 g after correction for dilution factors. Analytical reliability was ensured by blanks, replicate injections, and standard reinjections, while peak identification was confirmed by retention time and spike-and-recovery tests (Jin et al., 2022; Sakaguchi et al., 2023).


#### Antioxidant activity of various malts and beers


#### Sample preparation

To prepare malt extract, 10 g of ground malt was combined with 100 mL of distilled water and extracted at 100 °C for 1 h. The extract was filtered and freeze-dried (FDU-1200; Tokyo Rikakikai Co., Ltd., Japan), and the beer was freeze-dried. The prepared samples were stored in a − 80 °C freezer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for use.


#### 2,2-diphenyl 1 picrylhydrazyl (DPPH)

DPPH (0.2 mM solution) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was added to the samples and allowed to react for 30 min in the dark (Lee et al., 2023). To assess the DPPH radical scavenging activity of the samples, the absorbance was quantified at 517 nm using a microplate reader (INNO, LTEK) and compared to that of the control l-ascorbic acid (Sigma-Aldrich). The results were expressed as a percentage of DPPH radical scavenging activity and analyzed for their correlation with the concentrations of the samples used in the assay.


#### 2,2′-Azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS)

The ABTS radical scavenging activity assay was performed as previously described (Lee et al., 2023). Briefly, 7 mM ABTS (Sigma-Aldrich) and 2.45 mM potassium persulfate (Hayashi Pure Chemical Ind. Ltd., Osaka, Japan) were prepared and allowed to react for 24 h in the dark. The ABTS solution and the samples were mixed and incubated for 6 min in the dark. The absorbance of the sample was measured at 734 nm using a microplate reader (INNO, LTEK). l-ascorbic acid served as the control, and all experiments were conducted in triplicate. The results were expressed as a percentage of the ABTS radical scavenging activity and analyzed for correlation with the concentration of the samples used in the assay.


#### Reducing power

Reducing power was assessed according to the methodology outlined in Arunkumar et al. (2021). Samples were prepared by mixing 0.2 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.8) and 1 % potassium ferricyanide (Chameleon Analytical Reagent, Osaka, Japan), and kept at 50 °C for 20 min. Subsequently, 1 % trichloroacetic acid (w/v) was added and the mixture was centrifuged at 3500 ×g for 10 min. The supernatant was combined with a solution of 0.1 % ferric chloride in distilled water. The absorbance was measured at 700 nm using l-ascorbic acid as the reference standard. All experiments were performed in triplicates.


#### Analysis of total polyphenol, total flavonoids, and total tannin content

Total polyphenols, flavonoids, and tannins were quantified using modified spectrophotometric assays, as previously described by Lee et al. (2023) with slight modifications. Total polyphenol content was determined by mixing 25 μL of the sample extract with 500 μL of distilled water and 50 μL of 2 N Folin-Ciocalteu reagent. After incubating the mixture for 3 min in the dark, 500 μL of 20 % (w/v) sodium carbonate was added. The mixture was gently vortexed and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 1 h. Following incubation, 200 μL of the reaction mixture was transferred to a 96-well microplate, and the absorbance was measured at 765 nm using a microplate reader (INNO, LTEK, Korea). A gallic acid standard (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was prepared in serial dilutions (0–1000 μg/mL) to generate the calibration curve. The results are expressed as milligrams of gallic acid equivalent (GAE) per 100 g of dry extract. Total flavonoid content was assessed by adding 100 μL of the sample extract to 400 μL of distilled water and 30 μL of 10 % (w/v) sodium nitrite. After 5 min of incubation, 30 μL of 10 % (w/v) aluminum chloride was added, followed by 6 min of dark incubation. Subsequently, 200 μL of 1 M sodium acetate and 240 μL of distilled water were added. After mixing, the reaction was allowed to proceed for 11 min at room temperature in the dark, and 200 μL of the mixture was transferred to a microplate. The absorbance was measured at 510 nm using a microplate reader (INNO, LTEK, Korea). (+)-Catechin hydrate (Sigma-Aldrich) was used to prepare standard solutions ranging 0–1000 μg/mL, and the results were expressed as milligrams of catechin equivalents (CE) per 100 g of dry extract. The total tannin content was determined by mixing 1 mL of the sample extract with 1 mL of 95 % ethanol and 1 mL of distilled water. After thorough mixing, 500 μL of 1 N Folin-Ciocalteu reagent and 5 % sodium carbonate solution were added. The mixture was incubated for 1 h at room temperature and the absorbance was measured at 725 nm using a microplate reader. Tannic acid (Sigma-Aldrich) was prepared as a standard solution in concentrations ranging 0–1000 μg/mL, and the tannin content was expressed as milligrams of tannic acid equivalents (TAE) per 100 g of dry extract. All analyses were performed in triplicate, and the data are presented as mean values to ensure experimental reliability and reproducibility.


#### Aroma profile analysis with electronic nose

The aroma profiles of the beer samples were analyzed using a Heracles II electronic nose system (Alpha-MOS, Toulouse, France), which operates based on the principle of ultrafast gas chromatography (GC) coupled with dual flame ionization detectors (FIDs). The system is equipped with two columns of different polarities, a non-polar MXT-5 (10 m) and a medium-polar MXT-1701 (10 m), to enable effective separation of aroma profiles with a wide range of chemical properties. The operational parameters of the electronic nose used for beer analysis are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Data acquisition and profile identification were performed using Alpha Soft V12 software, and the aroma profiles were matched against the AroChemBase V4 HERACLES V12 chemical library based on their retention times and peak areas. This methodological setup allowed for rapid and reproducible profiling of volatile components, thereby enabling comparative analysis of aroma characteristics between different beer samples (Wiśniewska et al., 2016).


#### Electronic tongue analysis

The taste sensory profiles of malts and beers were determined using an ASTREE II electronic tongue (Alpha MOS). The electronic tongue consisted of three parts: an autosampler with a silver/silver chloride reference electrode, a sensor array or system, and an analyzer or data acquisition system. This study used sensors designed to detect sourness, saltiness, umami, sweetness, and bitterness. Specifically, AHS (sourness), CTS (saltiness), NMS (umami), ANS (sweetness), and SCS (bitterness) are used to detect taste, whereas PKS and CPS function as universal sensors. The sensor data was processed using Alpha Soft software (version 12; Alpha MOS).


#### Description of the sensory test

The sensory test was conducted by ten panelists (six males and four females, aged 22–34 years) at Sogang University, Seoul, Korea. All panelists had prior experience with sensory evaluation of fermented beverages. This study was approved by the IRB board (202309-HR-04). Prior to the formal evaluation, panelists underwent three training sessions (50–60 min each) to establish descriptors, develop definitions, and practice intensity scaling. During the first training, panelists tasted representative beer samples and discussed potential sensory descriptors. In the second training, reference standards were introduced to calibrate perception of the selected attributes, and the panelists compared and refined the terms through group discussion. In the third training, a pilot test was performed to ensure consistent understanding and scoring of each descriptor. Reference standards were prepared and provided to the panelists to anchor key sensory attributes, following previous reports (Lee et al., 2019; Sung & Lee, 2017; Hong et al., 2020; Song et al., 2024). For example, black sugar 100 % was used as the standard for “caramel aroma,” 0.25 % (w/v) citric acid solution for “sourness, 0.1% Anhydride caffeine for “bitterness,” alcohol solution (25 %, v/v) for “alcohol aroma/taste,” and carbonated water for “carbonation.” (Table S2). These standards were presented during training and again available in the evaluation booths to ensure consistency. Eighteen sensory attributes were finalized to characterize the beer samples (Table S3), based on international EBC/ASBC descriptive terminology. Each beer sample (20 mL) was served in a transparent plastic cup labeled with randomized three-digit codes. The serving order was randomized. Panelists were instructed prior to the evaluation and provided with drinking water. Each attribute was scored on a seven-point scale, where higher values indicated stronger intensity. The data were averaged across the ten trained panelists and used in the subsequent preference analysis.


#### Statistical analysis

All measurements were performed in triplicate, and the mean ± SD values are presented. Statistical analyses were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with the statistical software package SPSS (version 23.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Because roasting degree (base, light, dark) and malt type (base vs. liquid) were evaluated in separate experiments rather than in a full factorial design, comparisons were analyzed independently for each factor. Differences among roasting degrees (base, light, dark) and between malt types (base vs. liquid) were assessed by Tukey's post-hoc test at a significance level of p < 0.05, (*: p < 0.05, **: p < 0.01, ***: p < 0.001). In addition, Tukey's test was employed to evaluate the significance of differences (p < 0.05, p < 0.01, p < 0.001) in the heatmap and correlation analyses. Principal component analysis (PCA) plots were generated using the Alpha-MOS program, and OriginPro 2024 (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA) was used to construct correlation plots and heatmaps.


### Results & discussion


#### Characteristics of malts

Table 1 lists the compositions of roasted and liquid malts. The color of the light and dark barley malt darkened as it was roasted from the base; liquid malts were darker than that of the base malt. The moisture content of the light and dark malts exhibited a decline relative to that of the base malt, contingent on the degree of roasting. In contrast, the liquid malt exhibited the highest moisture content among the various malts. The study revealed that base malt exhibited the lowest levels of soluble nitrogen among the various roasted malts. The Kolbach indices of the base, light, dark, and liquid malts were calculated as the ratio of soluble nitrogen to total nitrogen. This study revealed that all malts exhibited similar levels of soluble nitrogen. The crude ash and carbohydrate content increased in the order of base, light, and dark as the roasting process progressed. Moreover, a positive relationship was identified between diastatic power and the carbohydrate content. The crude ash and carbohydrate contents demonstrated an increase in conjunction with the availability of specific nutrients following heat treatment (Magpantay et al., 2020). Furthermore, the increase in reducing sugars due to carbohydrate breakdown during roasting facilitated the Maillard reaction (Food and Agriculture Organization [FAO], 1998). Diastatic power (i.e. malt enzyme activity) facilitates starch breakdown into fermentable glucose (Charmier et al., 2021), dextrins, and maltose. The diastatic power should decrease due to enzyme inactivation at high temperatures (Charmier et al., 2021). However, our research results showed that the diastatic power increased with the addition of roasted malts under specific roasting conditions. In addition, most of the characteristic values of the liquid malt were significantly lower than those of the base malt. The liquid malt displayed intermediate diastatic power relative to those of the base and light malts (Table 1). This result demonstrates that a small amount of enzyme activity was maintained during the roasting process (Coghe et al., 2005). Total sugar content increased with degree of roasting. The liquid malt had significant total sugar content compared to base malt (Table 1). Because reducing sugars are used as precursors for thermally generated compounds, it has been suggested that heat application during kilning increases sugar content in the final product (Lekjing & Venkatachalam, 2020). In this study, the pH of the malt was analyzed according to the degree of roasting. As roasting progressed, the pH increased significantly, likely owing to the degradation of acidic organic compounds and the accumulation of basic high-molecular-weight compounds formed through the Maillard reaction. A similar trend has been reported in coffee roasting, where the titratable acidity initially increases and then decreases over time, which is attributed to the breakdown of organic acids and the formation of reactive products (Nikiforova et al., 2024). In addition, liquid malt, which was measured in its concentrated form without dilution, had the lowest pH of 4.69 (Table 1). This was likely because of the higher concentration of water-soluble acidic compounds, such as organic acids, acidic peptides, and polyphenols, which remained concentrated after hot water extraction and evaporation (Cheng et al., 2021). Melanoidin and Amadori compounds are formed via the Maillard reaction during roasting (Hellwig & Henle, 2020). The highest concentrations of melanoidin were observed in dark malt (Fig. S1A). The levels of Amadori compounds in the malt increased depending on the degree of roasting (Fig. S1A). Additionally, the melanoidin and Amadori compounds content of the liquid malt showed higher value than those of the base beer (Fig. S1B). Moreover, the melanoidins of dark malt beer exhibited significant value than those of the base beer (Fig. S1C). The Amadori compounds in beer significantly increased only in beer made with dark malt (Fig. S1C). There were notable differences in the base malt and liquid malt beer (Fig. S1D).

The relative change in the free amino acid content of beer as roasted malts and malt type was added. To ensure transparency and data reproducibility, the original chromatograms together with the triplicate measurement values have been provided in the supplementary file (Fig. S2 and Table S4). These chromatograms confirm the presence and separation of the major amino acid peaks consistent with the quantified values, thereby supporting the accuracy of our processed data. In addition, the free amino acid changes were expressed through hierarchical cluster analysis (Fig. 1F). The content of serine, tyrosine proline, phenylalanine, cystathionine, and γ-aminobutyric acid increased by roasting processing. In addition, the threonine, glutamic acid, glycine, alanine, lysine, arginine, ethanolamine, histidine, and ornithine contents in light malt beer were the highest in the sample groups. However, the values of arginine and cystine decreased during the roasting process, and the heatmap of the liquid displayed a darker blue color for phenylalanine, lysine, arginine, and ornithine contents compared to the base. Serine interacts with methionine to form a volatile profiles that is involved in the aroma and flavor of beer (Yin et al., 2017). Glutamic acid plays a role in the umami flavor of beer (Schmidt et al., 2021). Therefore, the umami taste in beer is expected to be stronger in light and dark beers using roasted malt than in those using the base. They concluded that in mild reactions, the composition of amino acids and sugars is the most important factor in aroma profiles, and proline, alanine, and tyrosine are involved in the reaction; however, it has been reported that most amino acids, except proline, are lost during the beer brewing process (Ferreira & Guido, 2018). Therefore, we observed that glycine, alanine, and tyrosine are lost during beer brewing, and the high proline content of light- and dark-roasted malt is due to the Maillard reaction. Also, cystathionine is synthesized by the enzyme cystathionine β-synthase, encoded by the CYS4 gene, which helps to improve beer quality by reducing the amount of hydrogen sulfide, which causes off-flavors in beer (Astola et al., 2023). In the present study, the cystathionine content increased with the addition of roasted malt, suggesting that beer with roasted malt is likely to have better properties. Leucine and phenylalanine increased the concentrations of higher alcohols and esters in the beer (Lin et al., 2022). Alcohol and esters are key contributors to the flavor and aroma of beer (Kucharczyk et al., 2020). The addition of roasted and liquid malt appeared to enhance these sensory properties by increasing leucine and phenylalanine content, which serve as precursors for the synthesis of higher alcohols and esters involved in flavor development (Pires & Brányik, 2015). It was reported in Yilmaz and Toledo (2005) that histidine is a Maillard reaction product with radical scavenging activity. Therefore, histidine in beer with roasted malt is likely to be a Maillard reaction product that affects radical scavenging activity.


#### Antioxidant activity and Maillard-derived profile contributions

The antioxidant activity of beers brewed with different malts was evaluated using DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging and reducing power assays (Fig. 2A–D). All samples showed concentration-dependent increases in antioxidant activity, with dark malt beer demonstrating the highest DPPH scavenging at 1000 μg/mL (Fig. 2A). Also, liquid malt beer had significant activity compared to base malt beer (Fig. 2A). Similarly, ABTS radical scavenging activity was strong in light and dark beers, particularly at 500 and 1000 μg/mL, comparable to the positive control (Fig. 2B). The ABTS activity of liquid malt beer exhibited similarly high values (Fig. 2B). Notably, the ABTS radical scavenging activity was higher than the DPPH radical scavenging activity across all samples (Fig. 2A and B). This discrepancy is likely because of the greater stability of the ABTS assay and its different reactivities with beer components. Specifically, polyphenols, proteins, and melanoidins may interact differently with DPPH radicals, influencing the measured values (Martinez-Gomez et al., 2020). Furthermore, polyphenol–protein interactions in beer may increase turbidity, thereby affecting absorbance-based assays such as DPPH (Zhao et al., 2021). The reducing power assay showed a similar trend, with light and liquid malt beers exhibiting significantly higher reducing capacities, again indicating a greater presence of electron-donating profiles such as Maillard reaction products (Fig. 2C). Among the beer samples, liquid malt beer exhibited higher antioxidant activity than base malt beer for all methods, indicating the potential contribution of Maillard reaction-derived compounds. These include melanoidins and Amadori products, which are known to possess antioxidant properties (Hellwig & Henle, 2020). This is consistent with the findings of Nardini and Foddai (2020), who reported that beers produced with roasted malts, including cocoa and nuts, demonstrated a 15–30 % increase in DPPH and ABTS activities. In our study, liquid malt beer showed a 27.3 % increase in DPPH scavenging activity and a 24.8 % increase in ABTS activity compared to the base beer, indicating that optimized roasting enhances radical-scavenging capacity. The total polyphenol and flavonoid contents increased with the degree of roasting, particularly in the light and dark malt beers (Fig. 2D). However, tannin content showed no significant change (Fig. 2D). Although liquid malt beer had relatively low levels (Fig. 2D), likely owing to dilution, its antioxidant capacity remained high, supporting the functional role of the Maillard-derived profiles. These results are consistent with those of Nardini and Foddai (2020), who reported enhanced phenolic content and antioxidant activity in beer made from roasted ingredients, including nuts, cocoa, and green tea. Recent studies have also reported a strong correlation between total polyphenol and flavonoid content and antioxidant capacity in beer, with correlation coefficients of >0.9 in some cases (Piazzon et al., 2010). This further supports the view that the phenolic profiles are critical contributors to the functional properties of beer. In addition, Chung et al. (2012) and Hellwig and Henle (2020) demonstrated that Maillard reaction products, including melanoidins and Amadori compounds, enhance antioxidant activity and contribute to beer color. Our results support this mechanism, showing that beers containing higher levels of these components, especially dark and liquid malt, exhibit stronger antioxidant properties. Moreover, in contrast to Fumi et al. (2011) who observed phenolic degradation during brewing with maize adjuncts, our data indicates that well-optimized roasting of barley malt can preserve or even improve phenolic stability and bioactivity. This highlights the importance of malt composition and processing conditions in the development of functionally enhanced beers.

According to the Venn diagram results (Fig. 3B), light malt beer shared the highest aroma profiles with the base beer, particularly esters and alcohols, indicating that volatiles are strongly preserved under mild thermal conditions. The profiles of ethyl hexanoate, cis-3-hexenyl acetate, and 2-octenal are significantly elevated in light malt beer, contributing to fruity, floral, and green sensory notes (Giannetti et al., 2019; Parr et al., 2021; Prado et al., 2021). This interpretation was reinforced by a heatmap correlation analysis (Fig. 3C), which showed that light malt beer exhibited the highest degree of positive correlation with base beer across nearly all profile classes. These profiles were also closely clustered in Fig. 4, highlighting their shared chemical classifications and sensory functions, and thereby reinforcing their contributions to the fruity and floral attributes of light beer. The strong associations, quantified by correlation coefficients ranging from −1 to +1 and statistically validated (p ≤ 0.05, p ≤ 0.01, p ≤ 0.001), indicate that light roasting effectively maintains the structural and sensory integrity of the volatile matrix (Giannetti et al., 2019; Johnson et al., 2012).

Dark malt beer retained a moderate number of shared volatiles, but also exhibited an increase in Maillard-derived profiles. Specifically, dark malt beer exhibited elevated levels of 2,3-dimethylpyrazine, trimethylpyrazine, benzaldehyde, furfural, and maltol. These profiles are classic Maillard reaction products that contribute to the roasted, nutty, caramelized, and bready-colored aromas in thermally processed foods and beverages (Delgado-Andrade, 2016; Starowicz & Zielinski, 2019). Similar patterns were observed by Féchir et al. (2021), who reported that dark specialty malts had increased pyrazine levels by >40 %, thereby enhancing the roasted aroma. Our GC-based electronic nose analysis confirmed that dark malt beer had a 1.7-fold higher total pyrazine signal intensity than the base malt beer, indicating a congruent aroma reconfiguration because of Maillard intermediates. Owing to the intensified thermal treatment, Strecker degradation also contributes to the increased presence of aldehydes and heterocyclics (Hellwig & Henle, 2020). The correlation heatmap (Fig. 3C) showed a partial loss of connectivity in dark malt beer compared to the base beer, particularly among the ester and alcohol groups, indicating a breakdown in volatile network integrity. As shown in Fig. 4, hierarchical clustering further revealed a shift toward pyrazines, ketones, and phenolic aldehydes, aligned with the darker flavor tones of heavily roasted malts. This convergence of aroma profiles in darker malts supports the hypothesis that high roasting intensity leads to the simplification and reconfiguration of the volatile matrix. Such transformations are characterized by the thermal formation of Maillard products, such as pyrazines, furans, aldehydes, and furanones, which are widely recognized for their roasted and caramel-like notes (Starowicz & Zieliński, 2019; Delgado-Andrade, 2016). These findings indicate that dark malt undergoes controlled volatile reconfiguration, while maintaining a reasonably cohesive aromatic structure, which is consistent with previous reports on flavor enrichment using Maillard intermediates (Hellwig & Henle, 2020; Wang et al., 2022).

Liquid malt beer had the fewest unique aroma profiles and the most divergent profile (Table 2), likely because of the thermal degradation or transformation of glycosidically bound volatiles during concentration. As illustrated in the correlation heatmap (Fig. 3C), this sample showed widespread disruption of volatile associations, particularly among esters and monoterpenes. The hierarchical clustering results (Fig. 4) further confirmed fragmentation of volatile groupings, with reduced levels of esters (ethyl acetate, ethyl hexanoate, and ethyl octanoate) and monoterpenes (γ-terpinene, α-terpinene) forming separate, less cohesive clusters. These changes may have resulted from glycosidic cleavage or thermal decomposition at the different malt concentrations. Notably, the significant decrease in γ-terpinene, a with known antioxidant and citrus aroma properties, suggests its susceptibility to thermal degradation during kilning and brewing. Although direct evidence in beer is limited, previous studies have reported that γ-terpinene can undergo oxidative or dehydrogenative transformation into p-cymene under aerobic or thermal conditions (Asikainen et al., 2013; Poulose & Croteau, 1978). This trend is further supported by Mahanta et al. (2021), who reported that thermolabile terpenes such as γ-terpinene rapidly degrade in citrus essential oils under thermal processing above 80 °C. Although their study was not beer-specific, it supports the general thermal lability of γ-terpinene. In our study, γ-terpinene levels dropped by over 90 % in dark and liquid malt beers, highlighting the need to balance aroma retention with thermal processing. Consequently, these reactions not only reduce its direct aromatic contribution but may also facilitate the formation of Maillard-derived antioxidant profiles, indirectly enhancing beer stability.


#### Modulation of aroma profile and antioxidant-linked volatiles in beers brewed with different roasting degrees and malt types

As shown in Fig. 5A and Fig. S4, several of the volatile shifts correspond to changes in the antioxidant activity. Despite the diminished aromatic coherence, dark malt beer exhibited the highest DPPH, ABTS, and reducing power values, likely because of the abundance of Maillard-derived profiles, such as melanoidins, pyrazines, and nitrogen-containing volatiles. In contrast, liquid malt beer showed the lowest antioxidant capacity based on DPPH, ABTS, and reducing power assays (Fig. 5A), indicating that extensive thermal processing may degrade both volatile and nonvolatile antioxidant contributors. However, this trend was not consistent across all metrics. Mechanistically, the aroma profile formation in roasted malts occurs through Maillard pathways, involving the condensation of amino acids and reducing sugars to form Amadori products, which are dehydrated to produce reactive carbonyls. These intermediates then generate furans, pyrazines, and aldehydes through cyclization and rearrangement (Hodge, 1953; Nursten, 2005). This increase in pyrazine concentration agrees with the results of Féchir et al. (2021), who reported a significant increase in 2,3-dimethylpyrazine and trimethylpyrazine levels in beer brewed with dark-roasted malt. In our study, a similar pyrazine enrichment was observed, particularly in dark and liquid malt beers, confirming that intensified roasting enhances the formation of these Maillard-derived volatiles. Notably, several of these profiles, such as those of pyrazines and sulfur-containing volatiles, were nitrogen-rich, reflecting their origin in amino acid degradation. Nitrogen-containing volatiles such as dimethyl trisulfide and acetylpyrazine play key roles in savory, roasted, and caramel-like notes and exhibit functional properties, including antioxidant activity (Hellwig & Henle, 2020; Olaniran et al., 2017). Volatile interactions are influenced by the thermal input as well as precursor composition and matrix effects. For example, the appearance of dimethyl trisulfide in dark and liquid malt beers indicates intensified sulfur amino acid degradation or thiol exchange reactions, which add to the savory depth of flavor (Olaniran et al., 2017).


#### Electronic tongue and sensory evaluation of beers brewed with different roasting degrees and malt types

Electronic tongue analysis was performed using the ASTREE II system (Alpha MOS). Unlike chromatographic techniques, this instrument directly provides numerical sensor response values rather than chromatograms. Accordingly, the triplicate measurement values are provided in the supplementary file (Table S6). These results confirm the reproducibility of the electronic tongue measurements and support the differentiation of taste attributes among beers brewed with different roast degrees and malt types. Fig. 5B illustrates the 3D PCA score plot of barley beer analysis, showing that specialty beers were separated in three-dimensional space by PC1 (62.76 %), PC2 (30.07 %), and PC3 (5.3 %). Clusters were formed based on malt type and roasting conditions. Notably, roasting time and caramelized malt were the primary contributors to PC1 and PC2. The electronic tongue profiles revealed significant differences among specialty beers. PC1 was dominated by PKS, AHS (sourness), SCS (bitterness), and CPS, while PC2 was associated with CTS (saltiness), NMS (umami), and ANS (sweetness). The flavor profiles analyzed by the electronic nose (Table 3) provided further insights. The base beer exhibited a prominent response to AHS, while light beer showed stronger responses in ANS and PKS, highlighting its sweeter characteristics. In contrast, dark beer was dominated by bitterness (SCS) and CPS. Beers brewed with liquid malt closely resembled the base beer but displayed higher saltiness (CTS) and umami (NMS) while showing reduced sweetness (ANS). These flavors were complemented by pronounced bitterness (Prado et al., 2021; Reichel et al., 2021). Research indicates that less than 50 % of the total aroma profiles in beer are directly derived from malt, with the majority resulting from other processes during brewing (Féchir et al., 2021). A sensory evaluation was conducted by ten graduate student panelists (four female and six male), assessing 18 organoleptic attributes, including appearance, flavor, taste, mouthfeel, and overall impression (Fig. S5). The analysis revealed that brightness decreased with increased roasting intensity. Dark beers scored higher in attributes such as bitterness, umami, carbonation, body, and viscosity, while light beers were sweeter. Beers made with liquid malt showed a profile similar to the base beer but exhibited higher sweetness and umami with lower sourness. Radar maps (Fig. S5) highlight the differences in sensory characteristics. Flavor analysis of light and dark roasted malt beers revealed a superior overall score for the latter. Liquid malt beer demonstrated enhanced sweetness, malty, sulfur, and fruity notes compared to the base beer, excluding nutty flavors. Mouthfeel analysis confirmed that light and dark beers had higher viscosity and astringency than the base beer, with liquid malt contributing to an increase in body weight and viscosity.

The results in Fig. 5C illustrate the correlation between sensory analysis and electronic tongue data using a chord diagram. Umami, sourness, and sweetness detected by the electronic tongue correlated strongly with the sensory test results, with umami and sweetness showing a particularly high agreement (99.84 % and 87.32 %, respectively). Additionally, bitterness and sourness values were found to correlate with saltiness in the sensory test. Some discrepancies between electronic tongue results and sensory data were attributed to the low gas selectivity of MOS-type sensors, which react to multiple gases simultaneously (Modesti et al., 2022). Modern brewing methods continue to explore new malt products with distinctive aromas and flavors. Smoked malts developed from apple and cherry woods impart light, fruity notes to beer, enhancing its sensory appeal (Deki & Yoshimura, 1974; Parker, 2015). This study highlights that roasted malts significantly influenced the flavor and sensory properties of beer, with dark roasted malts enhancing bitterness and umami while liquid malt increased sweetness.


### Conclusions

This study evaluated the effects of malt roasting degree and malt type on the physicochemical, antioxidant, and sensory qualities of beer. Roasting altered malt composition-enhancing carbohydrates, diastatic power, and Maillard reaction products, such as Amadori compounds and melanoidins, thereby influencing beer color, alcohol content, and yeast viability. Beers made with light- and dark-roasted malts showed higher antioxidant activity (DPPH, ABTS, and reducing power), which was associated with increased polyphenols and Maillard-derived aroma profiles such as pyrazines, furans, and maltol. Aroma profiling revealed that light malts retained fruity volatiles, whereas darker malts produced roasted notes linked to nitrogen-containing profiles, such as 2,3-dimethylpyrazine and dimethyl trisulfide. Thermal degradation of γ-terpinene emphasized the trade-off between volatile retention and antioxidant formation. These findings highlight the role of thermal processing in modulating the formation of bioactive compounds and aroma profiles. Thus, controlled roasting can serve as a strategic approach for the development of beers with enhanced functional and sensory properties.
